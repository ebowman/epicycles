\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph*{In what sense the hypothesis holds.}
The statement ``planetary motion is a system of epicycles'' is true in four
distinct and increasingly weak senses. (i)~For an unperturbed bound Kepler
orbit the heliocentric position is \emph{exactly} a Fourier series in the
mean anomaly, uniformly convergent for every $e<1$, and each term, a vector
in an orbital plane, is the sum of two counter-rotating circles
(Props.~\ref{prop:series} and~\ref{prop:twocircles}). (ii)~The geocentric
position is the sum of two such series, one for the body and one for the
Earth, so it is an epicycle system with radii and rates fixed by the orbital
elements. It is, however, not a Fourier series in the strict sense: the two
mean motions are in general incommensurate, so the function is Bohr
almost-periodic, with frequency module $\mathbb Zn_b+\mathbb Zn_\oplus$. There
are no cross terms at combination frequencies in the Cartesian geocentric
vector, because the difference is linear; numerically the amplitudes at
combination frequencies are $10^{-16}$ to $10^{-15}$ relative, with a single
line at $8.4\times10^{-11}$, and the out-of-mask $\sin^8$ window leakage
is $3\times10^{-8}$. (iii)~On
an analytic invariant torus of a near-integrable system, under the
assumptions of Eq.~\eqref{eq:quasi}, the position is again exactly and
uniformly a (multi-frequency) epicycle sum for all time; KAM theory supplies
such tori only under additional hypotheses and for a subset of initial
conditions, and does not show that a given body lies on one. (iv)~For any other
trajectory, such as our N-body Ceres integration, a finite set of epicycles
recovered by frequency analysis is an empirical approximation on a finite
window. The Ceres experiment demonstrates reconstruction accuracy there, with
interior errors that decrease with the number of lines (monotonically for $K\ge10$), and shows
its price, namely window-edge errors that do not; it does not demonstrate that
the motion is quasi-periodic.

The representation is exact only in cases (i)--(iii); in the cases flagged in
the theory it fails, or is only a finite-window approximation. For unbound orbits ($e\ge1$) the series does not exist. For
chaotic motion the frequencies drift and a fixed set of circles is valid
only on a finite time. Over long windows secular drift of the elements
limits any fixed chain, and in practice the resolution of two lines is
set by a scale of about $2\pi/T$ for a record of length $T$ (a conditioning
scale, not an absolute limit). High eccentricity is
also expensive: about $450$ harmonics per orbit are needed for
$10^{-9}$~AU at $e=0.89$ (sampled). The order certified by the bound grows as
$(1-e)^{-3/2}$, but this is not the asymptotic behaviour of the actual minimal
order, which tends to a finite limit at fixed tolerance as $e\to1$
(Table~\ref{tab:nearpar}). Finally, the Cartesian bound does not bound the
angular error near close approaches, where it scales as $1/\Delta$.

\paragraph*{``Epicycles can fit anything.''}
Hanson's observation \cite{Hanson1960} and its philosophical consequence
\cite{Carman2010} are correct for an arbitrary curve, and a data-driven
frequency analysis is, in that sense, a fit; our NAFF chains are fitted. For
the analytic construction the objection does not apply: every radius and
rate follows from $(a,e,i,\omega,\Omega,M_0)$ of the two orbits, and nothing is
adjusted to the geocentric data. The number of circles is a truncation
parameter with a rigorous error bound, not a freedom to absorb discrepancies.
This answers the objection only for the dynamically derived expansion, not for
Ptolemy's own free parameters, and the NAFF case is a measurement of
the spectrum of a trajectory rather than an independent test.

\paragraph*{Relation to prior work.}
As Sec.~\ref{sec:prior} describes, the conceptual core is Gallavotti's
\cite{Gallavotti2001}, the Bessel expansion is classical
\cite{Dutka1995,Kapteyn1893}, and the numerical FFT route is that of Peralta
et al.\ \cite{Peralta2003}; modern ephemerides are likewise sums of uniform
circular motions \cite{Bretagnon1988,Kudryavtsev2016}. Our results are a
quantitative, explicitly geocentric and three-dimensional assembly of these
ideas, with truncation bounds and a check of NAFF against the analytic
terms.

\paragraph*{Limitations.}
(1)~We do not validate against JPL Horizons or any observed ephemeris; the
reference for Kepler tests is exact two-body motion and for Ceres an
N-body integration with only the Sun, the Earth, Jupiter and Saturn.
(2)~The planetary elements are approximate J2000 mean elements and no light
time, aberration, precession or nutation is modelled, which changes the
numbers but not the structure of the argument. (3)~The models in
Table~\ref{tab:ptolemy}, including the circular two-term approximation A, are harmonic truncations of the Kepler series, not
reconstructions of Ptolemy's models. (4)~The NAFF edge effects are
significant: over the full window the extracted chain is no better than a
fitted Kepler orbit, and our tolerance check on the numerical reference is a single tighter rerun, which shows insensitivity to the tolerance rather than proving convergence. (5)~Truncation error is not monotone in the retained set
for small orders. (6)~Several historical sources were consulted only through
secondary accounts (Sec.~\ref{sec:history}).
